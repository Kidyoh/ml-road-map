questions:
  - prompt: "The central limit theorem worked for uniform draws and failed for Cauchy draws. Explain what condition the Cauchy violates, and name a real dataset where you should worry about the same thing."
    answer_key: >-
      The central limit theorem requires the underlying distribution to have a
      finite mean and finite variance. The Cauchy has neither — its tails are so
      heavy that the integral defining the mean does not converge — so averaging
      more samples does not stabilise anything. The average of n Cauchy draws is
      itself Cauchy with the same spread, no matter how large n gets, which is why
      your histogram never narrowed and your sample mean jumped between seeds.
      Real data with this flavour: financial returns during a crisis, wealth and
      income at the top of the distribution, file sizes, city populations, damages
      in insurance claims, and time-to-resolution for support tickets where a few
      cases run for years. In all of these the sample mean is unstable — it is
      dominated by whether your sample happened to include one extreme value —
      and confidence intervals built on normal assumptions are meaningless. The
      practical defence is to look at the median and other quantiles, which stay
      well behaved, and to plot the distribution before trusting any average.
  - prompt: "You simulated exponential waiting times and got Poisson counts. Explain the relationship between those two distributions, and say what assumption both of them make that real event streams usually violate."
    answer_key: >-
      They are two views of the same process. If events arrive independently at a
      constant average rate, then the number of events in a fixed window is
      Poisson with mean equal to rate times window, and the gap between
      consecutive events is Exponential with that same rate. Counting and waiting
      are the same information described from different ends. The assumption both
      make is that the rate is constant and the events are independent — that an
      event happening now tells you nothing about when the next one comes. Real
      event streams almost always break this. Website traffic has daily and weekly
      cycles, so the rate is a function of time. Support tickets cluster because
      one outage generates fifty of them. Purchases cluster because one customer
      buys three times in a session. When events cluster, the observed variance
      exceeds the Poisson mean — overdispersion — and the standard fix is a
      negative binomial, or splitting the window so the rate is roughly constant
      within each piece.
  - prompt: "Someone hands you a column of data and asks which distribution to model it with. Describe how you would actually decide, and say why 'it looks normal in the histogram' is a weak reason."
    answer_key: >-
      Start from the process, not the picture. Ask what generated the number. Is
      it a count of things that either happened or did not, out of a known number
      of tries — that is binomial. A count of events in a window with no natural
      upper bound — Poisson, or negative binomial if it clusters. A waiting time —
      exponential or Weibull. A quantity that is a sum of many small independent
      contributions — plausibly normal. A quantity that is a product of many
      factors, or is bounded below by zero and skewed right — log-normal. A
      proportion bounded in zero to one — beta. Knowing the mechanism narrows the
      candidates before you look at any data. The histogram is a check on that
      reasoning, not a substitute for it, and it is a weak check for three
      reasons: a small sample looks roughly bell-shaped under many distributions,
      the histogram says almost nothing about the tails, which is exactly where
      the choice matters most, and bin width changes the apparent shape. If you do
      want to test the fit, a QQ plot against the candidate is far more informative
      than a histogram, because it shows tail behaviour directly.
  - prompt: "Explain the difference between a probability density and a probability, for a continuous distribution. Then say what goes wrong if you forget it."
    answer_key: >-
      For a continuous variable the probability of any single exact value is zero
      — there are uncountably many possible values, so no individual one can carry
      positive mass. The density is not a probability; it is a probability per
      unit of x, and you get an actual probability only by integrating it over an
      interval. That is why a density can exceed 1: a distribution tightly
      concentrated on a narrow range has a tall density, and the area under it
      still comes to 1. What goes wrong if you forget: you interpret a likelihood
      value as a probability and get confused when it exceeds one, or you compare
      densities across variables with different units and conclude something
      meaningless, since changing from metres to centimetres divides every density
      by a hundred. In machine learning specifically, the log-likelihood a density
      model reports is in nats per unit of x, so comparing two models is only
      valid if they are modelling the same variable in the same units — a real
      source of nonsense comparisons between generative models.

## Why this node exists

Almost every model you will fit is a statement about a distribution. Linear
regression assumes normally distributed errors with constant variance. Logistic
regression models a Bernoulli. A loss function is usually a negative
log-likelihood in disguise. When a model behaves badly, the reason is often that
its distributional assumption does not match the data, and you cannot notice that
if distributions are just names to you.

This node is short on theory and long on simulation, because the fastest way to
know what a distribution does is to generate a hundred thousand draws from it and
look.

## The session

### 1. Five processes, five distributions

For each, simulate it, then match it to the closed form and confirm the numbers.

| Process | Distribution | Check against |
|---|---|---|
| 20 coin flips at p = 0.3, count heads | Binomial(20, 0.3) | mean 6.0, variance 4.20, P(X≤4) = 0.2375 |
| Emails per hour, average 3 | Poisson(3) | P(X=0) = 0.0498, P(X≤1) = 0.1991 |
| Gaps between those emails | Exponential(3) | mean 1/3 |
| Heights in a population | Normal | 68.27% within 1σ, 95.00% within 1.96σ |
| Something with monstrous tails | Cauchy | has no mean at all |

Use `scipy.stats` for the exact values and your own `rng` for the simulation. The
point is to watch them agree.

### 2. Counting and waiting are the same thing

Simulate exponential inter-arrival times with rate 3, accumulate them into a
timeline, then count how many events fall in each unit interval.

Those counts should be Poisson(3). Confirm the mean, and confirm that the
variance is also about 3 — the Poisson's defining oddity is that its mean and
variance are equal, which is exactly the property real clustered data violates.

### 3. The central limit theorem, and its limits

Draw 30 samples from `Uniform(0, 1)`, take the mean, repeat 100,000 times.
Histogram the result. It is normal, centred at 0.5, despite nothing in the
uniform being remotely bell-shaped. This is why the normal shows up everywhere.

Now do exactly the same with `rng.standard_cauchy()`.

It will not converge. Run it twice with different seeds and watch the sample mean
move by more than 1.0. This is the most important twenty seconds of the node: the
central limit theorem is a theorem with hypotheses, and heavy-tailed data
violates them.

### 4. Break each assumption

For each of the five, name a real column that would violate it:

- Binomial assumes a fixed number of independent trials with constant p. What
  breaks it?
- Poisson assumes a constant rate and independent events.
- Exponential assumes memorylessness — the chance of an event in the next minute
  does not depend on how long you have already waited.
- Normal assumes symmetric, finite-variance, additive noise.

The checklist asks for one concrete example each. This is the part that transfers.

## A table worth keeping

| If the quantity is... | Try | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Successes out of n trials | Binomial | trials not independent |
| Events per window, unbounded | Poisson | clustering → overdispersion |
| Events per window, clustered | Negative binomial | |
| Time until an event | Exponential, Weibull | non-constant hazard |
| A sum of many small effects | Normal | heavy tails |
| A product of many factors | Log-normal | |
| A proportion in [0, 1] | Beta | mass exactly at 0 or 1 |

## Common walls

**Your simulated variance is off by a factor of n/(n-1).** `np.var` defaults to
the population variance and `pandas.Series.var` to the sample variance. At 200,000
draws the difference is invisible; at 20 it is not.

**Your Poisson simulation from exponential gaps has too few events in the last
window.** You truncated the timeline mid-interval. Drop the final partial window.

**Your Cauchy histogram is unreadable.** It has values in the thousands. Clip the
x-range for display — but do not clip the data before computing the mean, or you
will have quietly turned it into a distribution that does have one, and destroyed
the entire lesson.

**You cannot tell whether your CLT histogram is "normal enough".** Use a QQ plot
against the normal rather than eyeballing the histogram. The tails are where the
answer is, and the histogram is worst exactly there.
