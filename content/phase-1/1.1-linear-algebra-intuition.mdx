questions:
  - prompt: "Explain what `A @ B` does to a vector, in terms of what each matrix does on its own. Then say why the order matters, using a concrete pair of transformations."
    answer_key: >-
      A matrix is a linear transformation of space, and the product is the
      composition of two of them: `(A @ B) @ v` applies B first, then A. The
      right-hand matrix acts first, which is the opposite of the reading order and
      is a common source of confusion. Order matters because composing
      transformations is not commutative in general. Take a 90-degree rotation and
      a scaling that stretches only the x-axis by 3. Rotate then stretch: a vector
      pointing up becomes a vector pointing right, and then gets stretched, so it
      ends up three times as long along x. Stretch then rotate: the up-pointing
      vector is unaffected by an x-only stretch, then rotates to point right at
      its original length. Same two operations, different results. The cases where
      order does not matter are special and worth recognising: two scalings along
      the same axes, two rotations in 2D, anything with the identity, and a matrix
      with itself.
  - prompt: "Your determinant-zero matrix collapsed the plane onto a line. Describe what that means for solving `Ax = b`, and connect it to something you will meet when fitting a linear model."
    answer_key: >-
      A determinant of zero means the transformation destroys a dimension — the
      output lives in a lower-dimensional subspace than the input, so different
      inputs map to the same output and the transformation cannot be undone. For
      `Ax = b` there are then two possibilities and no third: if b happens to lie
      on that line, infinitely many x produce it; if b lies anywhere off it, no x
      does. Either way there is no unique solution and no inverse. This is exactly
      what perfect multicollinearity is in a regression. If one feature column is
      a linear combination of others — a one-hot encoding with every level kept
      and an intercept, say, or a column duplicated under a different name — then
      the design matrix is singular, `X^T X` cannot be inverted, and the
      coefficients are not identified. Near-singularity is worse in practice than
      exact singularity, because it does not raise: the fit succeeds and returns
      enormous coefficients that flip sign when you resample the data.
  - prompt: "The eigenvectors of your matrix landed on the same lines after the transformation while other vectors rotated. Say what that property is useful for, without using the words 'eigenvector' or 'eigenvalue' in your explanation."
    answer_key: >-
      They are the directions the transformation does not turn — it only stretches
      or shrinks along them. That matters because it gives you a coordinate system
      in which the transformation is as simple as it can possibly be: instead of
      every axis mixing into every other, each direction just scales by its own
      factor. Anything you want to do repeatedly becomes easy in that frame,
      because applying the transformation a hundred times is just raising each
      scaling factor to the hundredth power. It also tells you what a repeated
      process converges to: the direction with the largest factor dominates
      everything else, which is why it describes the steady state of a Markov
      chain and the ranking behind PageRank. In data, if the transformation is a
      covariance matrix, these directions are the axes along which the data varies
      independently, ordered by how much variation each carries — which is the
      whole of principal component analysis.
  - prompt: "You will spend the rest of this roadmap reading shape errors. Describe your procedure for debugging one, and say what the shape of a gradient must always be."
    answer_key: >-
      The procedure: print the shape of every input to the failing line, then
      write down the shape you expected the output to be and why. Almost every
      shape error is one of four things — a transpose you forgot, an axis you
      summed over that you meant to keep, a batch dimension that got dropped by
      indexing with an integer instead of a slice, or a dimension of size one that
      broadcasting silently expanded. Reading the error message for the two shapes
      it names and asking which of the four it looks like resolves most of them in
      seconds. The rule about gradients is worth memorising because it catches
      errors before they happen: the gradient of a scalar with respect to a
      tensor always has exactly the same shape as that tensor. A loss is a scalar,
      so the gradient of the loss with respect to a (784, 128) weight matrix is
      itself (784, 128). If you have computed something with a different shape,
      you have not computed that gradient, whatever the numbers look like. Node
      4.1 leans on this constantly.

## Why this node exists

You do not need to prove theorems about vector spaces. You do need to stop
treating `A @ B` as an incantation.

Every shape error you will hit in the next six phases is a linear algebra
question wearing a stack trace. Every time a model's weights are a matrix — which
is always — the question of what that matrix *does* to its input is the question
of what the layer means. And PCA in node 3.4, attention in node 4.5 and the whole
of backpropagation in node 4.1 are unreadable if matrices are just grids of
numbers to you.

Two hours of 3Blue1Brown is genuinely the highest-return time in this phase. Do
not skip it on the grounds that you have seen matrices before.

## The session

### 1. Matrix multiply, three ways

Implement `A @ B` yourself, three different ways, and check all three against
numpy with `np.allclose`:

1. **The triple loop.** `out[i, j] = sum over k of A[i, k] * B[k, j]`.
2. **Row by column.** Each output entry is the dot product of a row of A with a
   column of B. One loop less.
3. **Sum of outer products.** `out = sum over k of outer(A[:, k], B[k, :])`.

The third one is the one worth sitting with. It says the product is a sum of rank-
one pieces, one per shared dimension — and that is the sentence that makes the
singular value decomposition make sense later.

### 2. Shapes, predicted

Write down the output shape *before* running each:

```python
(50, 30) @ (30, 20)
(3, 1) @ (1, 4)
(3,) @ (3,)
(5, 3) @ (5, 3)          # what happens, and why?
A.T @ A                  # for A of shape (100, 5)
```

The inner dimensions must match and they vanish; the outer ones survive. That is
the whole rule.

### 3. Transformations you can see

Take a grid of points covering the unit square, apply a 2x2 matrix, and plot
before and after. Try each of:

```python
np.array([[3, 0], [0, 0.5]])       # scaling
np.array([[1, 1], [0, 1]])         # shear
np.array([[0, -1], [1, 0]])        # rotation
np.array([[2, 4], [1, 2]])         # determinant zero - watch it collapse
```

For each, compute the determinant first and predict what will happen to the area.
Then look.

The fourth one is the important one. Plot it and watch two dimensions become one.

### 4. Eigenvectors, seen rather than solved

For `A = [[2, 1], [1, 3]]`:

```python
w, v = np.linalg.eig(A)
```

Check that the eigenvalues sum to the trace (5.0) and multiply to the determinant
(5.0). Then plot each eigenvector, apply A to it, and plot the result. It should
land on the same line through the origin, scaled.

Try five other vectors. They will not.

### 5. PCA by hand

Generate a 2D cloud that is obviously elongated in some direction:

```python
rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
base = rng.normal(size=(500, 2)) * [3.0, 0.6]
theta = np.deg2rad(30)
R = np.array([[np.cos(theta), -np.sin(theta)], [np.sin(theta), np.cos(theta)]])
cloud = base @ R.T
```

Centre it, compute the covariance matrix, take its eigenvectors, and plot the
principal direction over the scatter. It should point along the long axis — which
you rotated to 30 degrees, so you know the answer.

That is PCA. Node 3.4 adds vocabulary and nothing else.

## Common walls

**Your triple loop disagrees with numpy in the last decimal.** That is floating
point summation order, not a bug. `np.allclose` exists for this. If the
disagreement is larger than about 1e-10, it is a bug.

**`(3,) @ (3,)` returns a scalar and confuses you.** A 1D array in numpy is
neither a row nor a column; `@` between two of them is the dot product. This
ambiguity is the source of an enormous number of shape bugs. Being explicit with
`(3, 1)` and `(1, 3)` costs nothing and prevents them.

**Your eigenvectors look wrong because they point the other way.** An eigenvector
times minus one is the same eigenvector. Only the line matters, not the arrow.

**Your PCA direction is 90 degrees off.** You took the eigenvector with the
smaller eigenvalue. `np.linalg.eig` does not sort; `np.linalg.eigh` for symmetric
matrices returns ascending order, so the principal direction is the *last*
column, not the first.

**Your covariance matrix is the wrong shape.** `np.cov` assumes variables are
rows, not columns. Pass `rowvar=False` for the usual (n_samples, n_features)
layout, or transpose. Getting this wrong on a (500, 2) cloud gives you a 500x500
matrix, which is a memorable way to learn it.
