questions:
  - prompt: "State your baseline, your final number, and your noise estimate. Then answer honestly: was the modelling work worth it?"
    answer_key: >-
      The honest answer is frequently no, and saying so is the mark of the node
      landing rather than of the project failing. Node 2.2 produced a model that
      beat a single sorted column by 0.0006 AUC; node 2.6 produced one that beat
      a linear model by 0.12. Both are real outcomes and only one of them
      justifies the model. What makes the answer meaningful is that all three
      numbers exist: without the baseline there is nothing to compare against,
      and without the noise estimate a difference of two points is
      indistinguishable from luck. If the modelling was not worth it, the correct
      conclusion is not that the project failed — it is that you now know the
      signal in this data is simple, that a rule can be shipped instead of a
      model, and that further effort belongs on data or framing rather than on
      algorithms. That finding took a day and would otherwise have been
      discovered after a quarter of tuning.
  - prompt: "What would you do with ten times the data? Be specific about what changes and what does not."
    answer_key: >-
      The answer must come from the learning curve, not from intuition. If the
      curves have converged, ten times the data changes nothing and saying so is
      the whole answer — the constraint is the features or the framing, and more
      rows of the same thing will not move it. If the validation curve is still
      rising, more data helps, and the useful version of the answer estimates by
      how much from the curve's slope. What tends to change with an order of
      magnitude more data: rare classes and rare slices become modellable, so
      problems that had to be aggregated can be split; higher-capacity models
      become viable where they previously overfit; and finer-grained features,
      particularly high-cardinality categoricals, stop being noise. What does not
      change: a mislabelled target stays mislabelled, a leaky feature stays leaky
      at scale, a badly framed problem stays badly framed, and any bias in how the
      data was collected is reproduced faithfully at ten times the volume. Ten
      times more of a biased sample is a more confident wrong answer.
  - prompt: "Name something your system does badly and a situation where it should not be used. Explain why volunteering this is a professional strength rather than a weakness."
    answer_key: >-
      Any honest limitation — poor performance on a particular segment, a
      dependence on a data source that could disappear, a training population that
      does not match some group of real users, a latency profile unsuitable for
      interactive use, an inability to explain individual predictions where a
      regulation requires it. Volunteering it is a strength for three reasons.
      First, the limitations exist whether or not you name them, and the only
      question is whether they are discovered by you now or by a user later; the
      second is far more expensive and damages trust in everything else you built.
      Second, stating the boundary is what makes the rest of the claim credible —
      a system described as working everywhere has not been evaluated carefully
      enough to know where it does not. Third, it lets other people make good
      decisions: someone deciding whether to build on your work needs the failure
      modes more than the headline metric. The professional norm worth adopting is
      that a model card or limitations section is part of the deliverable, not an
      apology appended to it.
  - prompt: "Your reviewer found something. What was it, did you agree, and what does their finding tell you about what you cannot see in your own work?"
    answer_key: >-
      Any specific finding is fine; what the question tests is whether the review
      was real. The most common things a reviewer catches that the author cannot:
      a leak the author has looked at so many times it has become invisible, an
      assumption stated in the author's head but not in the README, a baseline
      that was not as naive as claimed, an eval set that only contains cases the
      system passes, and a README that makes sense only to someone who already
      knows the project. The general point is that authorship destroys the ability
      to read your own work as a stranger would — you cannot un-know the context,
      so you cannot tell what is missing from the page. That is exactly the same
      reason node 4.2 required two independent implementations to agree and node
      1.2 required a gradient checker written before the thing it checks: an
      independent perspective catches a class of error that no amount of care from
      inside can. Disagreeing with a review point is legitimate; declining it
      without a written reason is not.

## Why this node exists

Everything before this was scaffolded. The problem was chosen for you, the data
was handed to you, the checklist told you what correct looked like, and the
answer key was one click away.

None of that is available here, and that is the entire point. The capstone is the
first evidence that you can do this rather than follow this.

## What is required

From the build plan, verbatim:

> a self-sourced dataset, a baseline that was beaten, a hand-written eval set, a
> deployed endpoint, a monitoring dashboard, and a README explaining what they
> would do with ten times the data. Peer reviewed.

### The dataset must be yours

Not Kaggle. Not sklearn. Not a benchmark.

Scrape something. Pull an API. Use your own records — your git history, your
spending, your running data, your organisation's tickets. Collect it yourself.

The reason is that curated datasets have had every hard decision made for them
already: what a row is, what the label means, which rows were excluded, what to
do about the ambiguous cases. Making those decisions badly, and then discovering
you made them badly, is most of the skill.

Expect the data collection to take longer than the modelling. That ratio is
correct and it is what the job is actually like.

### A baseline, established first

Node 2.1's discipline. Write the number down before you model, in the README, and
do not move it afterwards.

### An eval set of at least 50 hand-labelled cases

Node 5.4's rule, unchanged: model-generated cases do not count. Include cases the
system gets wrong.

### Deployed, at a URL

Nodes 6.1 and 6.2. Someone else must be able to call it. The curl command goes in
the README and it must work when they run it.

### Monitored, and tested by breaking something

Node 6.5. The dashboard is not the deliverable — the deliverable is having broken
a component deliberately and watched the monitoring catch it.

### A README that is honest

- What this is and what decision it changes
- Baseline, final number, and the noise estimate
- What it does badly
- Where it should not be used
- What you would do with ten times the data, from your learning curve

## The peer review

**Required.** Not optional, not "if you can find someone".

Find another person — someone else working through this roadmap, a colleague, a
friend who codes. Give them this node's checklist. Ask them to go through it and
write down what they find.

Then address every point in writing, including the ones you disagree with.
"Declined, because X" is a complete answer. Silence is not.

Question 4 asks what they caught, and there will be something. Authorship
destroys your ability to read your own work as a stranger would — it is the same
reason node 4.2 required two implementations to agree.

## A checklist of the things that go wrong here

Drawn from every phase of this roadmap:

| | Node |
|---|---|
| Features computed across the cutoff | 2.3, 3.1 |
| No baseline, so no idea whether the model helped | 2.1 |
| Accuracy reported on an imbalanced problem | 2.4 |
| Preprocessing fitted outside the pipeline | 2.3, 3.2 |
| Threshold left at 0.5 without a reason | 2.4 |
| Eval set containing only passing cases | 5.4 |
| Model loaded per request | 6.1 |
| Deployed `latest` with no rollback | 6.2 |
| Cannot reproduce a result from last month | 6.3 |
| No alert for data that stopped arriving | 6.5 |

Your reviewer should check every row.

## Scope, honestly

The most common failure of a capstone is being too ambitious and finishing
nothing. A small, complete, deployed, monitored, honestly-documented system beats
an impressive half-built one, and it beats it by a wide margin — because the
second one demonstrates nothing that this roadmap taught.

If you must cut, cut model sophistication. Keep the baseline, the eval set, the
deployment, the monitoring and the README. Those are the parts that are hard to
learn and the parts nobody skips twice.

## After

You have finished. A few things worth doing with that:

- Write up what surprised you. The list is more valuable than you think, and it
  will be shorter than you expect.
- Go back to a node you rushed. Node 2.3 is the usual answer.
- Review someone else's capstone. Reviewing teaches differently from building.
- Then pick a problem that matters to you and do it again without the roadmap.
