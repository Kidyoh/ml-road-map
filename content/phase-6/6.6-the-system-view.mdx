questions:
  - prompt: "Show your diagram's riskiest path and explain why it is the riskiest. What makes a component dangerous beyond simply being important?"
    answer_key: >-
      Danger is not importance, it is the product of likelihood of failure,
      severity of consequence, and how long the failure goes undetected. A
      component can be critical and safe if it fails loudly and instantly — a
      model server that crashes takes the product down, which is severe, but
      everyone knows within seconds and the rollback is one command. The
      dangerous components are the ones that fail quietly and are trusted by
      everything downstream: a feature pipeline that starts producing subtly wrong
      values, a label pipeline that begins mislabelling, a join that silently
      drops rows. Those keep the system running while corrupting everything it
      produces, and the corruption propagates into the next retraining run and
      becomes permanent. On my diagram that is the feature computation path,
      because it is used by both training and serving, it has no schema
      enforcement between the two, and a discrepancy there produces no error at
      any point. The general test: for each component, ask what happens if it
      returns plausible but wrong output, and how long before somebody notices.
  - prompt: "Where in your system could training and serving compute a feature differently? Describe the mechanism by which that discrepancy stays invisible."
    answer_key: >-
      Anywhere the same feature is implemented twice — typically once in a batch
      job written in SQL or pandas for training, and once in the serving path
      written in application code, because the serving path needs it computed for
      one row in milliseconds while the training path computes it for millions in
      a nightly run. The two implementations start identical and diverge, because
      only one of them gets updated when a definition changes. It stays invisible
      because there is no point at which the two are compared: training reads its
      own features, serving computes its own, and nothing joins them. Every metric
      you have is computed within one world or the other, so both look fine. It
      surfaces as a model that validates well and performs worse in production for
      no apparent reason — a symptom easily misattributed to drift, which is why
      it can persist for a long time. The defences are to share one implementation
      between both paths, which is the core argument for a feature store; to log
      the features actually used at serving time and periodically compare them
      against what the training pipeline would have produced for the same entity;
      and to treat any difference as a bug rather than as noise.
  - prompt: "One of your failure modes takes more than a week to detect. Name it, and say what you would change to shorten that — including what the change would cost."
    answer_key: >-
      The usual candidate is concept drift, or any degradation whose only evidence
      is model performance, because performance needs labels and labels arrive on
      the business's schedule rather than yours. In fraud that is the chargeback
      window, which can be weeks; in churn it is the subscription period. Slow
      label corruption is the other one, since a labelling process that starts
      producing wrong answers is only detectable by auditing labels, which nobody
      does routinely. To shorten it: find a proxy that arrives sooner — analyst
      overturn rate on flagged cases, customer complaint volume, the prediction
      distribution itself — and monitor that instead, accepting that a proxy is
      noisier and can mislead. Or buy labels faster by paying humans to label a
      sample continuously, which converts a detection delay into an ongoing cost
      and is often worth it. Or hold out a small random slice from the model's
      decisions so you keep observing unbiased outcomes, which costs you the value
      of the decisions you deliberately did not make. Every option trades money or
      performance for detection speed, and the right choice depends on what a week
      of being wrong actually costs — which is a number worth computing rather
      than assuming.
  - prompt: "You broke a component and compared reality to your prediction. What did you get wrong, and what does that suggest about pre-mortems in general?"
    answer_key: >-
      Any honest answer is good; the common surprises are that detection was much
      slower than expected because the alert existed but was routed somewhere
      nobody watches, that the failure propagated further than predicted because a
      downstream component retried and amplified it, or that something failed
      first that was not on the diagram at all — a credential, a DNS entry, a disk
      filling up. What it suggests about pre-mortems is that they are necessary
      and not sufficient. They are excellent at enumerating failures you can
      imagine, which is more than most teams ever write down, and they
      systematically miss the ones that come from components you did not think
      were part of the system. That is why the last checklist item is to actually
      break something: an untested belief about how your system fails is a belief,
      and the gap between the two is where incidents live. The mature version of
      this is regular deliberate failure injection — game days, chaos engineering
      — for the same reason node 6.4 insists you watch the gate refuse something.

## Why this node exists

The Sculley paper this node points at makes one argument, and it is the argument
of the whole phase: **the model is a small part of a machine learning system.**
Everything around it — data collection, feature extraction, verification,
serving, monitoring, configuration — is larger, less examined and where the
failures live.

You have now built all of those pieces. This node is about seeing them as one
thing, and being able to say where it breaks.

## The session

### 1. Draw it

One page. Everything from raw data to a monitored prediction:

```
data source -> ingestion -> validation -> feature computation
                                              |
                          +-------------------+-------------------+
                          |                                       |
                    training path                          serving path
                          |                                       |
                    train -> evaluate -> gate                 model server
                          |                                       |
                       registry  ------------------------->  loads version
                                                                  |
                                                             predictions
                                                                  |
                                                    logging -> monitoring -> alerts
                                                                  |
                                                          labels (later) -> performance
```

**Label every arrow** with what flows and how often. "Features, hourly" and
"Features, per request" on two arrows out of the same box is the whole
training-serving skew problem drawn in two labels.

### 2. Find the duplicated transformations

Mark every place data is transformed. Then find the ones that happen in more than
one place.

That set is where training-serving skew lives. Question 2 asks how the
discrepancy stays invisible, and the answer is that nothing ever compares the two.

### 3. The pre-mortem

Assume it is six months from now and the system has failed badly. Write the
incident report from the future.

At least five distinct failure modes, each with a **mechanism** rather than a
component name. Not "the feature pipeline broke" but "a schema change upstream
renamed a column, the join silently produced nulls, the model treated nulls as
zero, and predictions shifted for the 12% of customers with that attribute."

For each, two more columns:

| Failure | How would I find out? | How long? |
|---|---|---|

At least one must take more than a week. If none does, you have not thought about
labels — question 3.

### 4. Rank by expected cost

Likelihood times impact. Not by how interesting the failure is, which is the
default ordering and is why interesting-but-rare failures get engineered against
while boring-and-likely ones take the system down.

### 5. Break something

Pick one component. Break it in a test environment.

Compare what actually happened against what you predicted — the failure, the
propagation, and the detection time.

Question 4 asks what you got wrong, and something will be wrong.

## Where these systems actually break

From the paper, and from experience, roughly in order of how often:

| | Why it hurts |
|---|---|
| **Data dependencies** | unversioned, unmonitored, and changed by people who do not know you depend on them |
| **Training-serving skew** | two implementations of one definition |
| **Feedback loops** | the model shapes its own future training data |
| **Configuration** | sprawling, untested, and able to change behaviour as much as code |
| **Undeclared consumers** | somebody built on your output without telling you |
| **Glue code** | most of the codebase, none of it interesting, all of it load-bearing |
| **Pipeline jungles** | accreted transformations nobody can safely remove |

Notice that "the model was not accurate enough" is not on the list.

## Common walls

**Your diagram is only the training pipeline.** Serving, monitoring and labels
are where the long-detection failures live. Draw them.

**Every failure is detected in minutes.** You have only listed loud failures.
The quiet ones are the dangerous ones — question 1.

**Your ranking puts an exotic failure first.** Multiply by likelihood. A
schema change upstream is far more probable than anything clever.

**You cannot break anything safely.** That is itself a finding, and probably the
top item on your list. A system you cannot test failure in is a system whose
failure behaviour is unknown.
