    answer_key: >-
      Each of those statistics collapses the data onto a single number by
      averaging something, and averaging is exactly the operation that destroys
      shape. The mean is the balance point and says nothing about how mass is
      arranged around it. The variance is the average squared distance from that
      point, so a tight cluster plus one far outlier can equal a broad even
      spread. Correlation measures how much the two variables move together on
      average, and a perfect curve can produce the same value as a noisy straight
      line because the curve's departures cancel. The regression line is fitted by
      minimising average squared error, which one leveraged point can dictate
      entirely — series IV is a vertical stack plus a single distant point, and
      that point alone determines the slope. The general lesson: every summary
      statistic answers one specific question, and the questions you did not ask
      are invisible in the answer. Plotting asks all of them at once.
  - prompt: "The overall mean reading is 23.953, and 271 readings out of 12,000 fall anywhere near it. Explain what has gone wrong with using the mean here, and describe the situation where you would notice this without a plot."
    answer_key: >-
      The distribution is bimodal — a day mode near 18.5 and a night mode near
      31.5 — so the mean sits in the trough between them, describing a state the
      sensor is almost never in. It is a real number correctly computed and it
      corresponds to nothing physical. The mean assumes the data has a single
      centre, and when it does not, the mean names an empty region. You could
      notice this without a plot, but only if you went looking: compare the mean
      against the median (close here, so no help), or against the mode, or check
      whether the standard deviation is large relative to the mean, or compute the
      fraction of points within one standard deviation and find it far below the
      68 percent a normal distribution would give. All of those are things you
      have to think to check. The histogram makes it unmissable in one glance,
      which is the entire argument for looking before summarising.
  - prompt: "532 of sensor S-03's readings are exactly 0.000. Say why a hard zero is a much stronger signal than a merely low reading, and describe how you would handle those rows in a model — including the option you think is wrong."
    answer_key: >-
      A physical measurement almost never lands on exactly 0.000 twice, let alone
      532 times. Continuous quantities are continuous; an exact repeated constant
      is a machine writing a default, not a world being measured. So the value is
      not a low temperature, it is a missing measurement wearing a number's
      clothes, and the fact that it is numeric rather than null is what makes it
      dangerous — every downstream mean, standard deviation and model input
      absorbs it silently. Handling: convert them to NaN first, so that they are
      at least honest, then decide. Dropping the rows is defensible if the
      dropouts are unrelated to what you are predicting. Imputing with the
      sensor's own median is defensible if they are random gaps. Adding an
      explicit `was_missing` indicator column is usually the best of the three,
      because the fact that a sensor failed is often itself informative. The wrong
      option is leaving them as zeros, and the second-wrongest is imputing them
      with the overall mean, which invents a value in the trough that the sensor
      never reports.

## Why this node exists

Most plots in most notebooks are decoration. They are made after the analysis, to
illustrate a conclusion already reached, and they are styled more carefully than
they are read.

This node is about the other kind: the plot you make *before* you know the
answer, whose entire purpose is to show you something you were not looking for.
That plot is usually ugly. It is also the one that catches the broken sensor, the
duplicated rows, the impossible date, and the distribution that makes your model
assumptions nonsense.

The two datasets here are rigged so that the summary statistics are innocent and
the picture is not.

## The session

### 1. Anscombe's quartet

[`anscombe.csv`](/datasets/anscombe.csv) — 44 rows, four series of 11 points,
published by Francis Anscombe in 1973 to make exactly this argument.

Before plotting anything, compute for each series: mean and variance of x, mean
and variance of y, the correlation, and the slope and intercept of the fitted
line. Put them in a table. They will agree to two decimal places.

Then plot all four as a 2x2 grid of scatter plots with the fitted line drawn on
each.

Describe each shape in one sentence. If you cannot, look longer — the whole
point is that these four are obviously different once seen.

### 2. The stuck sensor

[`sensor-readings.csv`](/datasets/sensor-readings.csv) — 12,000 readings from
four sensors over 24 hours.

Somebody hands you this and asks for the average temperature. Do not answer yet.

- Histogram `reading_c`, all sensors together. What do you notice about the
  overall mean once you have seen this?
- Histogram it again, one panel per sensor. One of them is different.
- Count exact zeros. Where are they?
- Now decide what the average temperature is, and be prepared to explain what
  you excluded and why.

### 3. Your own data

Load the dataframe you cleaned in node 0.3 and go looking. Not for anything in
particular — that is the exercise.

Suggestions if you are stuck: the distribution of `amount`; transactions per
customer; orders per day of week; whether `channel` behaves differently across
`segment`; whether anything is suspiciously round.

The checklist asks you to find at least one thing you did not know. There is
something there.

## The discipline being taught

For any new dataset, three plots before anything else:

1. **A histogram of every numeric column.** Catches bimodality, hard bounds,
   spikes at defaults, impossible values, log-normal shapes that want a
   transform.
2. **A bar chart of every categorical column's value counts.** Catches
   near-duplicate categories, unexpected levels, extreme imbalance.
3. **Counts over time, if there is a time column.** Catches gaps, a pipeline that
   broke for a week, a spike from a backfill.

That is fifteen minutes. It routinely saves days.

## On honest axes

A few rules worth holding to, because each corresponds to a way readers get
misled:

- **Bar charts start at zero.** The bar's length is the encoding; truncating the
  axis breaks the encoding. Line charts showing change over time do not have this
  constraint.
- **Label the axis with its unit.** "Temperature" is not a label; "temperature
  (°C)" is.
- **Say how many points there are.** A trend from 12 observations and a trend
  from 12,000 look identical and mean very different things.
- **Do not use a rainbow colormap for a continuous quantity.** It creates
  boundaries where the data is smooth. `viridis` is the default for good reasons.
- **Plot the distribution, not just the aggregate.** Every plot in this session is
  an argument for that one.

## Common walls

**Your Anscombe statistics do not match.** Check `ddof`. NumPy's `var` defaults to
the population variance, pandas' to the sample variance. The published quartet
figures use the sample version (`ddof=1`), which is what pandas gives you by
default and numpy does not.

**Your histogram looks like a single lump.** Your bin count is too low. The
default of 10 hides almost everything; try 50, then 100, and watch what appears
and disappears. Bin count is a parameter you should always try three values of.

**You cannot see the zeros in the histogram.** 532 out of 12,000 is 4 percent —
visible as a spike at the left edge, but easy to miss at low bin counts or with a
wide x-range. Try `df.reading_c.value_counts().head()` as well; an exact repeated
value shows up instantly in a value count and never in a distribution summary.

**Your 0.3 plots are all of aggregates.** Sums and means by group are the plots
you make when you already know the question. Plot the raw distribution first.
