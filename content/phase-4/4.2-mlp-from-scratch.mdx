  - prompt: "Your numpy and PyTorch losses matched to five decimal places for ten steps. Explain why that is a much stronger test than both models eventually reaching similar accuracy."
    answer_key: >-
      Two independent implementations agreeing on the exact trajectory can only
      happen if they compute the same forward values and the same gradients at
      every step. It is a test of the computation, not of the outcome. Eventual
      accuracy is a weak test because neural networks are extraordinarily
      forgiving: a gradient that is scaled by a constant, missing a term, or wrong
      for one layer will often still train to a respectable accuracy, just more
      slowly or to a slightly worse optimum — and you have no reference to notice
      the difference against. Whole categories of bug survive that test, including
      a misplaced factor in the softmax derivative, a bias gradient summed over
      the wrong axis, and an activation derivative applied to the post-activation
      value instead of the pre-activation one. Step-by-step agreement kills all of
      them immediately, and it localises the bug too: the first step where the
      losses diverge tells you which operation to look at.
  - prompt: "Derive the gradient of the softmax-plus-cross-entropy combination with respect to the logits, and say why frameworks implement them as a single fused operation."
    answer_key: >-
      For cross-entropy loss with a one-hot target, the gradient of the loss with
      respect to the logits is simply the predicted probabilities minus the
      one-hot target — softmax(z) - y. It is remarkably clean, and it comes from
      the softmax derivative and the log in the cross-entropy cancelling almost
      everything. Frameworks fuse them for two reasons. The first is numerical
      stability: computing softmax separately involves exponentiating logits,
      which overflows for large values, and then taking a log of the result, which
      underflows for small ones. The fused version applies the log-sum-exp trick,
      subtracting the maximum logit before exponentiating, so the intermediate
      never overflows and the log is never taken of a number rounded to zero. The
      second is efficiency: the fused backward is one subtraction rather than
      constructing the full Jacobian of the softmax, which is a matrix per sample.
      This is why PyTorch's CrossEntropyLoss takes raw logits and why applying a
      softmax before it is a common and quietly damaging mistake — it makes the
      gradients much smaller and training much slower without raising anything.
  - prompt: "You can state the shape of every intermediate tensor for a batch of 64. Walk through them, and say which single dimension convention causes the most bugs."
    answer_key: >-
      Input is (64, 784) — batch first, then flattened pixels. After the first
      weight matrix of shape (784, 128) the result is (64, 128); the bias is (128,)
      and broadcasts across the batch. After ReLU it is still (64, 128). After the
      second weight matrix (128, 10) it is (64, 10), which are the logits, one row
      of ten per image. The loss reduces that to a scalar. Every gradient has the
      same shape as the thing it is a gradient of, so dW1 is (784, 128) and db1 is
      (128,) — which is the invariant from node 1.1 and the fastest way to catch a
      mistake. The convention that causes the most bugs is the batch dimension:
      whether it comes first or last, and what happens when it disappears.
      Indexing with an integer rather than a slice silently drops it, turning
      (64, 784) into (784,), and everything downstream then broadcasts in a way
      that produces plausible shapes and wrong numbers. The bias gradient is the
      other reliable trap, because it must be summed over the batch axis and
      summing over the wrong one gives a shape that sometimes still works.
  - prompt: "Your network reaches 95%+ on MNIST. Explain why that number tells you almost nothing about whether your implementation is correct."
    answer_key: >-
      MNIST is very easy. Logistic regression on raw pixels reaches about 92%, a
      k-nearest-neighbour classifier gets around 97% with no training at all, and
      almost any two-layer network with roughly correct gradients will clear 95%.

      accuracy is dominated by the dataset rather than by the quality of the
      implementation. A network with a subtly wrong gradient — one scaled by a
      constant, or missing the bias term's contribution — will still get there,
      just after more epochs, and you would have no way of knowing. That is
      exactly why this node requires the step-by-step comparison against PyTorch
      and the gradcheck rather than an accuracy target: both are tests of
      correctness, and accuracy is a test of the dataset. The same caution applies
      to reading any benchmark number on a standard dataset, which is a habit
      worth carrying into node 5.4.

## Why this node exists

Node 4.1 gave you an autograd engine over scalars. Real networks operate on
tensors, and the step from one to the other is where most of the practical
detail lives — batching, shapes, the softmax-cross-entropy pairing, and
initialisation.

Writing it twice and demanding agreement is the point. One implementation is
never verified; two implementations that agree step by step are.

## The session

MNIST: 60,000 training images, 10,000 test, 28x28 grayscale, 10 classes.

### 1. Count the parameters first

Before writing anything:

| Layer | Weights | Biases | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 784 → 128 | 784 × 128 = 100,352 | 128 | 100,480 |
| 128 → 10 | 128 × 10 = 1,280 | 10 | 1,290 |
| | | | **101,770** |

And the deeper variant, 784 → 256 → 128 → 10: **235,146**. Work it out yourself
and check against the framework at the end.

### 2. The numpy implementation

```python
# forward
z1 = X @ W1 + b1        # (64, 784) @ (784, 128) -> (64, 128)
a1 = np.maximum(0, z1)
z2 = a1 @ W2 + b2       # (64, 128) @ (128, 10) -> (64, 10)
loss = softmax_cross_entropy(z2, y)

# backward - derive these yourself
dz2 = (softmax(z2) - onehot(y)) / batch_size
dW2 = a1.T @ dz2
db2 = dz2.sum(axis=0)
da1 = dz2 @ W2.T
dz1 = da1 * (z1 > 0)
dW1 = X.T @ dz1
db1 = dz1.sum(axis=0)
```

Every one of those lines is the chain rule from node 1.2, applied to arrays. The
`.sum(axis=0)` on the biases is the accumulation from node 4.1 — the bias
contributes to every row of the batch, so its gradient sums over them.

Gradcheck it on a small batch before training.

### 3. The PyTorch implementation

```python
model = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Flatten(),
    nn.Linear(784, 128),
    nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Linear(128, 10),
)
loss_fn = nn.CrossEntropyLoss()      # takes LOGITS. no softmax before it.
```

Then copy your numpy weights into it:

```python
with torch.no_grad():
    model[1].weight.copy_(torch.tensor(W1.T))    # note the transpose
    model[1].bias.copy_(torch.tensor(b1))
```

The transpose is not optional — PyTorch stores `Linear` weights as
(out_features, in_features), which is the opposite of the numpy convention above.

### 4. The test

Same initial weights, same batches, same learning rate, plain SGD. Print both
losses for ten steps.

They must match to five decimal places.

If they do not, the first step where they diverge tells you which operation to
look at. That localisation is worth more than any amount of staring.

### 5. Now train it

Baseline first: the most common MNIST class is 1, with 6,742 of 60,000, so
always guessing it gives about **11.24%** accuracy.

Train to at least 95% **test** accuracy. Question 4 asks you why that number is
much less impressive than it sounds.

## Initialization matters more than it looks

Initialise everything to zero and every hidden unit computes the same thing
forever — the gradients are identical, so they stay identical, and 128 units
behave as one.

Initialise too large and ReLU units saturate or the loss becomes NaN on the first
step.

The standard answer is He initialisation for ReLU: normal with standard deviation
`sqrt(2 / fan_in)`. For 784 inputs that is about 0.0505. Node 4.3 goes further
into why.

## Common walls

**Your loss starts at some huge number.** With 10 balanced classes an untrained
network should start near `ln(10) = 2.303`. Anything far above that means your
initialisation is too large or your loss is misconfigured. This is the single
best sanity check in deep learning and it costs one print statement.

**PyTorch's loss is much lower than numpy's from step one.** You applied softmax
before `CrossEntropyLoss`. It expects logits.

**Your bias gradient has the wrong shape.** Sum over the batch axis, `axis=0`,
giving shape (128,) not (64, 128).

**Weights copied but losses differ.** The transpose. PyTorch is
(out, in); the numpy code above is (in, out).

**Your accuracy is 9.8% and stays there.** Zero initialisation, or a learning
rate so large the first step destroyed the weights. Print the loss at step zero.

## Before you move on

Node 4.3 is about what happens when training does not work, and it introduces the
discipline that Appendix B calls the point of that node: overfit a single batch
to near-zero loss before you attempt anything real. It is also one of the three
nodes scheduled for an execution runtime, because self-verification here is
unusually untrustworthy.
