questions:
  - prompt: "Explain what the query, key and value matrices are each for, without using the words 'database' or 'lookup' unless you can cash out the analogy precisely."
    answer_key: >-
      Each position produces three different linear projections of its own
      representation, and they play three different roles. The query is what this
      position is looking for — it is compared against other positions to decide
      how much to attend to each. The key is what this position offers as a
      basis for being found — it is what queries get compared against. The value
      is what this position actually contributes once it has been attended to. The
      separation matters because what makes a position relevant and what it should
      contribute are different questions: a token might be highly relevant to
      identify — a subject noun that a later verb needs — while what it
      contributes is something quite different from the features that made it
      findable. If you used one projection for both, relevance and content would
      be forced to live in the same space and the model would be strictly less
      expressive. The database analogy does cash out: it is a soft lookup where
      instead of one exact key match you get a weighted average over all values,
      weighted by how well each key matches the query.
  - prompt: "Removing the sqrt(d) scaling made the softmax nearly one-hot and the gradients tiny. Explain the chain of reasoning from dimension to dead gradient."
    answer_key: >-
      A dot product of two d-dimensional vectors with independent entries of unit
      variance has variance d, so its typical magnitude grows like the square root
      of d. At d equal to 64 the raw scores are spread over roughly plus or minus
      8 rather than plus or minus 1. Softmax is exponential, so a spread that wide
      means the largest score dominates completely and the output is nearly
      one-hot. The problem is that the gradient of softmax is proportional to
      p(1 − p) terms, which vanish when p is near 0 or near 1 — so a saturated
      softmax passes almost no gradient back, and the attention weights stop
      learning. Dividing by sqrt(d) rescales the scores back to unit variance,
      keeping the softmax in the region where it is sensitive to its inputs. It is
      the same phenomenon as a saturated sigmoid in node 4.3, and the same class
      of fix as He initialisation: control the variance of what flows through the
      network so that the non-linearities operate where their derivatives are not
      zero.
  - prompt: "Attention with no positional encoding is permutation equivariant. Explain what that means concretely, and why it is a problem that needs fixing rather than a feature."
    answer_key: >-
      It means that if you shuffle the input positions, the outputs come back in
      exactly the same shuffled order, unchanged in content — the mechanism has no
      access to where anything is. Every position attends to every other by
      content only, and the score between two positions depends on their
      representations and not at all on their distance or order. That is a problem
      for language because order carries meaning: "the dog bit the man" and "the
      man bit the dog" contain identical multisets of tokens and mean different
      things, and an order-blind model cannot distinguish them. It is fixed by
      adding position information into the representations themselves, either with
      fixed sinusoidal encodings, learned position embeddings, or relative schemes
      such as RoPE that modify the attention scores by relative offset. It is
      worth noting that the property is genuinely useful when the input really is
      a set — molecular graphs, point clouds, sets of items — where imposing an
      arbitrary order would be a false assumption. Convolutions have the opposite
      bias, and that contrast is the whole architectural argument.
  - prompt: "Attention is O(n²) in sequence length while a convolution is O(n). Say what the quadratic cost buys, and when you would prefer the convolution anyway."
    answer_key: >-
      It buys a direct path between every pair of positions. In a convolution,
      information from position 1 reaches position 500 only by passing through
      many layers, each with a small receptive field, so long-range dependencies
      are learned indirectly, weakly, and only if the network is deep enough for
      the receptive field to span the distance. Attention connects them in a
      single operation with a learned weight, so the path length is constant
      regardless of distance — which is exactly why transformers handle long-range
      structure so much better and why they replaced recurrent models, whose path
      length is linear and whose gradients decay along it. What you pay is memory
      and compute scaling with the square of the sequence, which at long contexts
      dominates everything. You would prefer the convolution when the important
      structure genuinely is local — most of vision, audio at short timescales,
      time series where recent history matters most — because then the locality
      prior is true and you get it for free rather than spending capacity and
      compute learning it. The practical picture is that hybrids are common, and
      that a great deal of research goes into approximating attention at
      subquadratic cost.

## Why this node exists

Attention is the mechanism behind almost everything in phase 5, and it is
explained badly nearly everywhere — usually with an analogy about databases that
is never cashed out.

It is fifteen lines of arithmetic. Writing them yourself takes an hour and
replaces the analogy with a thing you know.

## The session

### 1. Scaled dot-product attention, from nothing

```python
def attention(Q, K, V, mask=None):
    d_k = Q.shape[-1]
    scores = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(d_k)          # (seq, seq)
    if mask is not None:
        scores = np.where(mask, scores, -np.inf)
    weights = softmax(scores, axis=-1)       # each ROW sums to 1
    return weights @ V, weights
```

Shapes, for seq_len 10 and d_model 64:

| | Shape |
|---|---|
| Q, K, V | (10, 64) |
| `Q @ K.T` | (10, 10) |
| weights after softmax | (10, 10) |
| `weights @ V` | (10, 64) |

Output shape equals input shape, which is what makes these blocks stackable.

Assert the softmax rows:

```python
assert np.allclose(weights.sum(axis=-1), 1.0, atol=1e-9)
```

### 2. Delete the scaling and watch it die

Run with and without the `/ np.sqrt(d_k)`, on random inputs at d = 64.

| | Max softmax probability | Largest gradient through softmax |
|---|---|---|
| With scaling | moderate, spread out | normal |
| Without | > 0.99, nearly one-hot | two orders of magnitude smaller |

Measure the gradient rather than assuming it. Question 2 asks for the chain of
reasoning, and it is the same saturation story as node 4.3.

### 3. Multiple heads

With `d_model = 64` and 8 heads, each head operates in **8** dimensions, not 64.
So the scaling factor is `sqrt(8) = 2.828427`, not `sqrt(64) = 8`.

Getting this wrong is a common and quiet bug — it does not raise, it just makes
attention slightly too sharp or too flat.

The heads run in parallel over disjoint slices of the representation, and their
outputs are concatenated back to 64 and passed through one more linear layer.

### 4. Causal masking

For a model that predicts the next token, position 3 must not see position 4.

```python
mask = np.tril(np.ones((seq, seq))).astype(bool)
```

Apply it **before** the softmax, setting the disallowed entries to `-inf` so they
become exactly zero after exponentiating. Masking after the softmax would leave
the rows not summing to one.

Verify: row 3 of the weights must be exactly zero in columns 4 through 9.

### 5. Attention cannot see order

Permute the input positions and run attention again. The output is the same,
permuted the same way. No positional information exists anywhere in the
mechanism.

Then add positional encodings and repeat the test — the equivariance breaks,
which is the point of adding them.

## Attention against convolution

| | Convolution | Attention |
|---|---|---|
| Prior | features are local, position-invariant | any position may matter to any other |
| Path length between distant positions | many layers | one operation |
| Cost in sequence length | linear | quadratic |
| Knows about order | yes, structurally | no, must be told |
| Parameters | shared across positions | shared across positions |

Neither is better. They encode different assumptions, and node 4.4's argument
applies: the right one is whichever assumption is true of your data.

## Common walls

**Your softmax rows do not sum to one.** You applied it along the wrong axis.
`axis=-1` — each query row is a distribution over keys.

**`-inf` produced NaN.** A whole row was masked out, so the softmax divided by
zero. Check your mask construction; every row must have at least one allowed
position, and the diagonal guarantees it for causal masks.

**Multi-head output has the wrong shape.** The concatenation must reassemble to
`d_model` exactly. With 8 heads of 8 dimensions, 8 × 8 = 64.

**Your attention weights are uniform.** At initialisation they should be roughly
uniform — that is correct, and it is what "the model has not learned what to
attend to yet" looks like.
