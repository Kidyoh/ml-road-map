questions:
  - prompt: "Your unlimited tree got 100% training accuracy and a worse cross-validated score than a depth-3 tree. Explain what the extra depth actually bought, in terms of what the tree is fitting."
    answer_key: >-
      It bought memorisation. Each additional split partitions the training data
      further, and with enough splits every training point ends up in its own pure
      leaf — at which point the tree reproduces the training labels exactly, by
      construction, having learned nothing generalisable. The deep splits are
      responding to noise: a leaf containing three patients whose particular
      measurements happened to line up is describing those three patients, not the
      relationship between measurements and diagnosis. On new data, the rule that
      isolated them is arbitrary. The depth-3 tree cannot do this — with only
      eight leaves it is forced to find splits that hold for large groups, which
      is the same thing as being forced to find real structure. This is the
      bias-variance tradeoff in its clearest form: the deep tree has almost no
      bias and enormous variance, and cross-validation measures the sum.
  - prompt: "Averaging 300 of those overfit trees produced a much better model than any one of them. Explain the mechanism, and say what would break it."
    answer_key: >-
      Each fully grown tree has low bias and high variance — it is right on
      average but wildly unstable. Averaging many unbiased high-variance
      estimators reduces variance while leaving bias alone, so the average is both
      approximately unbiased and much more stable. The catch is that averaging
      only reduces variance to the extent that the errors are independent: if all
      300 trees made the same mistakes, averaging would change nothing. That is
      why random forests inject two sources of randomness rather than one —
      bootstrap sampling of the rows, so each tree sees a different dataset, and
      random subsetting of the features at every split, so no single dominant
      feature is chosen first by every tree. The second matters more than it looks;
      without it, a strong feature makes every tree nearly identical near the root
      and the correlation between trees stays high. What breaks the whole scheme
      is anything that makes the trees agree — too few features to subsample, a
      feature so dominant that it is picked whenever it is offered, or trees so
      shallow they are all the same simple rule.
  - prompt: "Logistic regression beat the random forest on this dataset. Give a reason why that is not surprising, and say what would have to be true about a dataset for the forest to win."
    answer_key: >-
      The breast cancer features are continuous measurements of size, texture and
      shape, and the classes are close to linearly separable in that space — the
      relationship really is approximately "larger and more irregular means
      malignant", monotonic in most features. A linear model is exactly the right
      shape for that, and it uses every feature smoothly, whereas a tree has to
      approximate a smooth monotonic boundary with axis-aligned staircases, which
      costs it accuracy and data efficiency. With 569 rows, the forest is also
      operating on very little data. Forests win when the true relationship is not
      smooth or not monotonic: interactions that matter only in combination,
      thresholds with genuine meaning such as a legal age limit, features whose
      effect reverses over their range, and heterogeneous tabular data mixing
      categoricals with numerics on unrelated scales. They also win when you
      cannot be bothered to scale, transform or engineer anything, which is a real
      and underrated advantage. The lesson is that model families encode
      assumptions about shape, and the right one depends on the data rather than
      on which is newer.
  - prompt: "Your random noise column received a non-zero Gini importance, and permutation importance ranked it near zero. Explain why the two disagree, and say which you would report."
    answer_key: >-
      Gini importance, also called mean decrease in impurity, adds up how much
      each feature reduced impurity across every split it was used in, measured on
      the training data. A continuous random column has many distinct values, so
      it offers a great many possible split points, and with enough of them some
      split will reduce training impurity by chance — especially deep in the tree
      where the node contains few samples. So the metric rewards features for
      being high-cardinality and for being available to overfit with, which is a
      well-documented bias toward continuous and high-cardinality variables.
      Permutation importance instead measures the drop in a held-out score when
      that column's values are shuffled, so a column with no real relationship
      causes no drop and scores about zero. Report permutation importance, on
      held-out data, and be aware of its own limitation: when two features are
      correlated, shuffling one alone lets the model lean on the other, so both
      look unimportant. For anything consequential, use a method built for
      correlated features or group them before permuting.

## Why this node exists

Trees are the first model family in this roadmap that can memorise its training
data completely, which makes them the clearest possible demonstration of
overfitting. You will watch a model achieve a perfect score and get worse.

They are also the foundation of gradient boosting, which is node 2.6 and which is
still, for tabular data, usually the thing that wins.

And this node contains a second lesson that tutorials rarely deliver: the
ensemble is going to lose to logistic regression, and understanding why is worth
more than the win would have been.

## The session

`sklearn.datasets.load_breast_cancer()` — 569 rows, 30 features, 212 malignant
and 357 benign. Everything here is 5-fold stratified cross-validation with
`random_state=0`.

### 1. One tree, two depths

```python
DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=3, random_state=0)
DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0)          # unlimited
```

| | Leaves | Depth | Train accuracy | CV ROC AUC |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| depth 3 | 8 | 3 | 0.9789 | **0.9409** |
| unlimited | 22 | 7 | **1.0000** | 0.9210 |

The deeper tree is perfect on the data it saw and worse on data it did not. Print
the tree with `export_text` and look at the leaves near the bottom — they will
contain one or two samples each.

### 2. Three hundred trees

```python
RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300, random_state=0)
```

**0.9923** cross-validated ROC AUC. From 0.9210 to 0.9923 by averaging the same
kind of model that scored 0.9210.

Question 2 asks you for the mechanism. The short version: the individual trees
are unbiased and unstable, and averaging kills instability.

### 3. The comparison nobody runs

| Model | CV ROC AUC |
|---|---|
| Always predict benign | 0.5000 |
| Decision tree, depth 3 | 0.9409 |
| Decision tree, unlimited | 0.9210 |
| Random forest, 300 trees | 0.9923 |
| **Logistic regression, standardized** | **0.9955** |

The linear model wins. Run it yourself rather than taking this table's word for
it — the checklist asks you to.

This is not a trick dataset. It is a very common situation, and the habit of
checking the simple model is worth more than any tuning you will ever do.

### 4. Feature importance, and why to distrust it

Three experiments:

**Reseed.** Refit the forest with `random_state` 0 through 4 and compare the top
three features each time. The order will change. If a ranking moves when you
change a seed, it was never a ranking.

**Add noise.** Append a column of `rng.normal(size=569)`. It has no relationship
to anything. Look at its Gini importance — it will not be zero.

**Permute.** Compute `permutation_importance` on held-out data. The noise column
drops to about zero, where Gini importance gave it credit.

Question 4 asks why. The short version: Gini importance rewards features for
offering many places to split, and a continuous random column offers a great
many.

## How a tree actually splits

At each node it tries every feature and every threshold, and picks the one that
most reduces impurity:

```
Gini impurity = 1 - sum over classes of p^2
```

A node that is all one class has impurity 0. A 50/50 node has impurity 0.5. The
split score is the weighted impurity of the children subtracted from the parent's.

That is the entire algorithm. Everything else — depth limits, minimum samples
per leaf, pruning — exists to stop it running to purity, because running to
purity is exactly what produced your 1.0000 training accuracy.

## Common walls

**Your unlimited tree does not reach exactly 1.0000.** You have duplicate rows
with conflicting labels, or you set a `min_samples_leaf`. Check both.

**Your CV numbers differ slightly from the table.** Check that you are using
`StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)` and scoring `roc_auc`. Trees
are sensitive to fold composition on 569 rows.

**Your random forest takes a long time.** 300 trees on 569 rows should be under a
second. If it is not, you have `n_jobs=1` and a very slow machine, or you are
refitting inside a loop.

**You concluded that forests are worse than linear models.** On this dataset, on
this metric, with this much data. That is the correct scope of the conclusion,
and node 2.6 will give you a case where the ordering flips.

## Before you move on

Node 2.6 replaces averaging with sequential correction — each tree fixing the
previous ones' mistakes rather than voting independently. It is a small change
in idea and a large change in behaviour, including in how easy it is to overfit.
