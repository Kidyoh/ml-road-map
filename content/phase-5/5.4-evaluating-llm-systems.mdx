questions:
  - prompt: "Appendix B says synthetic or model-generated eval sets do not count. Give the argument, and then give the strongest counterargument and say why it loses."
    answer_key: >-
      The argument: a model-generated eval set encodes the generating model's idea
      of what the task is and what a good answer looks like. If you then evaluate a
      system built on the same or a similar model, you are measuring agreement
      between two models that share assumptions, blind spots and failure modes,
      not whether the system does the job. Generated cases cluster around the
      typical and the well-formed, so they systematically omit the ambiguous,
      badly-phrased, adversarial and unusual inputs that real users produce — and
      those are exactly the cases that break systems. The counterargument is
      practical and real: hand-labelling is slow, fifty cases is a small sample,
      and a generated set of five thousand covers more surface. It loses because
      coverage of the wrong distribution is not coverage. A large eval set that
      does not contain your actual failure modes will report a high score while
      the system fails in production, which is worse than a small honest one
      because it is actively misleading. The workable compromise is to use
      generation to draft candidates and then have a human read, edit and label
      every one — at which point the human labelling, not the generation, is what
      makes it an eval set.
  - prompt: "You labelled 10 cases twice and disagreed with yourself some number of times. Explain what that number means for every other metric in this node."
    answer_key: >-
      It is the noise floor of the entire evaluation. If I disagree with myself on
      two cases out of ten, then no measurement built on my labels can resolve
      differences smaller than roughly that, and any comparison between two
      versions of the system that differs by less than that is indistinguishable
      from relabelling noise. It also caps the LLM judge: a judge cannot agree with
      me more than I agree with myself, so a judge scoring 85% agreement against an
      80% self-consistency is at the ceiling rather than underperforming. Most
      usefully, self-disagreement points at the places where the rubric is
      underspecified — the cases I flip on are the ones where I had not decided
      what counts as correct, and tightening the definition improves both my
      consistency and the judge's. This is the same reasoning as inter-annotator
      agreement in any labelling task, and skipping it is why so many eval numbers
      are reported to two decimal places when the first one is not stable.
  - prompt: "You computed Cohen's kappa for your LLM judge rather than raw agreement. Explain the difference and why it matters here."
    answer_key: >-
      Raw agreement is the fraction of cases where the judge and I gave the same
      label. Its problem is that it does not account for agreement that would
      happen by chance, and chance agreement is high whenever the labels are
      unbalanced. If 90% of my cases are labelled correct, a judge that says
      "correct" to everything scores 90% raw agreement while carrying no
      information — which is node 2.4's majority-class classifier in a new place.
      Cohen's kappa subtracts the expected agreement under independent random
      labelling with the same marginal frequencies and rescales, so 0 means chance
      and 1 means perfect. A kappa of 0.6 to 0.8 is usually described as
      substantial and is about what a good judge achieves; below 0.4 the judge is
      not usable as a substitute for human labelling. It matters here specifically
      because eval sets are usually unbalanced toward passing, so raw agreement
      flatters every judge and would let me automate my evaluation on the strength
      of a number that means nothing.
  - prompt: "You made a change and the score moved. Describe how you decided whether the movement was real, and what you would do if it fell inside the noise."
    answer_key: >-
      By first measuring the noise floor: running the unchanged system twice and
      recording how much the score moved on its own, which for a sampled model is
      not zero even at temperature zero, per node 5.1. Any change smaller than
      that band is not evidence of anything. With 50 cases, one case is two
      percentage points, so the resolution is inherently coarse and a move of
      three or four points is within the range where a couple of borderline cases
      flipping explains it entirely. Beyond the noise floor, the right check is
      whether the same cases changed in the same direction rather than only
      whether the aggregate moved — a change that fixes three cases and breaks
      three others nets to zero and is not a null result, it is a trade you should
      look at. If the movement falls inside the noise, the honest options are to
      keep the change if it is better on other grounds such as cost or latency, to
      enlarge the eval set in the region the change was meant to affect, or to
      revert and try something with a larger expected effect. What you must not do
      is report it as an improvement, which is node 1.4's p-hacking wearing new
      clothes.

## Why this node exists

Appendix B could not be clearer: **the deliverable is a hand-labelled 50-case
eval set. Synthetic or model-generated eval sets do not count and the checklist
should say so plainly.**

It says so plainly.

Without an eval set, work on an LLM system is a sequence of prompt edits followed
by trying a few examples and forming an impression. That process cannot detect
regressions, cannot compare two approaches, and cannot tell you when to stop.
Everyone knows this. Almost nobody builds the eval set, because it is boring and
it takes a day.

## The session

### 1. Write 50 cases. By hand.

For node 5.3's RAG system. Each case is a question, the expected answer or the
criteria for a correct one, and any relevant metadata.

Deliberately include:

| Kind | Count |
|---|---|
| Cases the system currently gets right | some |
| **Cases it currently gets wrong** | at least 8 |
| **Cases where the corpus has no answer** | at least 5 |
| Badly phrased or ambiguous questions | some |
| Questions where two documents disagree | some |

An eval set containing only cases you pass measures nothing. The eight failures
are the most valuable rows in the file.

Do not generate these. Question 1 asks you to argue the rule, and the argument is
worth having in your own words.

### 2. Make it one command

```bash
python eval.py
```

Under five minutes, prints a score, writes per-case results to a file.

If running the eval is inconvenient, you will not run it, and an eval set you do
not run is a document rather than a tool.

### 3. Measure your own consistency

Relabel 10 cases a day later, without looking at your first labels.

Count the disagreements. That number is the noise floor for everything else in
this node, and question 2 explains why it caps even the judge.

### 4. Build a judge, then check it

An LLM that grades the outputs against your criteria. Then measure its agreement
with your labels — with **Cohen's kappa**, not raw agreement.

```python
from sklearn.metrics import cohen_kappa_score
```

Question 3 explains why raw agreement flatters every judge on an unbalanced set.

Find two cases where you and the judge disagree, and decide who was right. Both
outcomes are informative: if the judge was right, your labels need review; if you
were, your rubric needs tightening.

### 5. Establish the noise floor

Run the unchanged system twice. Record the score difference.

It will not be zero. That band is the resolution of your eval, and with 50 cases
each one is worth two percentage points.

### 6. Make one change and measure it

One change. Re-run. Compare against the noise floor.

Then look at *which* cases changed rather than only at the aggregate — a change
that fixes three and breaks three is a trade, not a null result.

## What to measure, by task shape

| Task | Metric |
|---|---|
| Extraction into a schema | exact match per field, plus schema validity |
| Classification | node 2.4's metrics, unchanged |
| RAG question answering | retrieval recall, answer correctness, citation accuracy |
| Summarisation | rubric scored by a judge, plus a factuality check |
| Open generation | pairwise preference against a baseline |
| Agents | task completion rate, steps taken, cost per task |

Prefer anything you can check programmatically. A regex is a more reliable grader
than a model and costs nothing to run.

## Common walls

**Your eval set is all easy cases.** You wrote questions you knew the system
handled. Go back to node 5.3's failure buckets and take cases from there.

**Your judge agrees with you 95% of the time.** Check the kappa. On an unbalanced
set this is what a judge that always says "pass" looks like.

**Fifty cases feels too small.** It is small, and it is enormously better than
zero. Grow it by adding every production failure you see — an eval set should
accumulate.

**The eval takes twenty minutes.** Use the batch API, parallelise, and cache
results per case so an unchanged case is not re-run. Node 5.1's cost arithmetic
applies.

## Before you move on

Node 5.5 builds agents, which are systems that call tools in a loop. Every
failure mode in this phase compounds there — an agent taking ten steps has ten
chances to go wrong, and without an eval set you will not know which step did.
