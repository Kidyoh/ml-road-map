questions:
  - prompt: "You found queries where BM25 beats dense retrieval. Characterise those queries, and explain what keyword search does that embeddings cannot."
    answer_key: >-
      They are queries containing rare exact tokens: product codes, error
      identifiers, version numbers, surnames, API method names, acronyms.
      BM25 matches terms literally and weights them by inverse document
      frequency, so a token appearing in three documents out of ten thousand is
      an extremely strong signal and the right document is retrieved with
      certainty. A dense embedding compresses the whole chunk into a few hundred
      dimensions, and a rare token contributes only a small nudge to that vector —
      it is not represented distinctly enough to dominate. Worse, an unfamiliar
      token may be split into subword pieces the model has no useful
      representation for. So dense retrieval reliably fails on exactly the queries
      where the user knows precisely what they want and has typed it. Embeddings
      win in the complementary case, where the user's words and the document's
      words differ but the meaning matches — synonyms, paraphrases, a question
      phrased differently from the answer. This complementarity is why hybrid
      retrieval is the default in serious systems rather than a refinement.
  - prompt: "Recall@1, recall@5 and recall@10 gave you three different numbers. Explain what each measures and which one your downstream system actually cares about."
    answer_key: >-
      Recall@k is the fraction of queries for which a relevant document appears
      somewhere in the top k results. Recall@1 asks whether the single best guess
      is right, which matters when only one result is shown or used. Recall@10
      asks whether the answer is anywhere in a page of results, which is a much
      easier bar and is what matters when a human will scan them. The one your
      system cares about is determined by how many results get passed on. For a
      RAG pipeline that puts five chunks into the context window, recall@5 is
      the number that bounds the whole system: if the answer is not in those five
      chunks, no amount of generation quality can recover it, and node 5.3's error
      attribution depends on knowing that boundary. Reporting recall@10 for a
      system that uses five chunks is a common and quiet form of overstating.
      Precision matters too, since irrelevant chunks consume context and can
      actively mislead the generator, which is why the k you choose is a real
      trade-off rather than "as many as fit".
  - prompt: "Re-chunking changed your recall. Explain the trade-off chunk size represents, and say what you would do if a single answer spans two chunks."
    answer_key: >-
      Small chunks are precisely targeted — the embedding represents one idea, so
      matching is sharp and the retrieved text is dense in relevant content — but
      they lose context, and a chunk that says "this is not recommended" without
      naming what "this" is cannot be interpreted alone. Large chunks preserve
      context but dilute the embedding: a chunk covering five topics has a vector
      that is the average of five things and is a strong match for none of them,
      so recall drops for specific queries. The trade-off is between precision of
      matching and sufficiency of context. When an answer spans two chunks, the
      standard remedies are overlap, so consecutive chunks share some text and
      neither is truncated mid-idea; retrieving neighbours automatically, by
      fetching the chunks adjacent to any hit; chunking on document structure such
      as headings or paragraphs rather than on a fixed character count; or
      indexing small chunks for matching while returning their larger parent
      section for context, which gets both properties and is usually the best
      answer. Chunking is underrated as a lever — it routinely matters more than
      the choice of embedding model.
  - prompt: "Approximate search was faster and slightly less accurate than brute force. Explain what the approximation gives up, and say at what corpus size you would switch."
    answer_key: >-
      Exact search compares the query against every vector, so it is guaranteed to
      find the true nearest neighbours and costs time linear in the corpus size.
      Approximate methods build a structure — an HNSW graph, an inverted file with
      clustering, or product quantisation — that lets a query examine only a small
      fraction of the vectors, which turns linear cost into something closer to
      logarithmic at the price of occasionally missing a true neighbour. The loss
      is usually very small, a percent or two of recall at reasonable settings,
      and it is tunable: the parameters trade recall against latency continuously.
      When to switch: below roughly ten thousand vectors, brute force with numpy
      is fast enough that the added complexity is not worth it, and it has the
      considerable advantage of being obviously correct. Between ten thousand and
      a million, an approximate index is worthwhile and the recall cost is
      negligible. Above that it is mandatory. The important discipline is to
      measure the recall loss against brute force on your own data before
      trusting the index, because the published benchmarks were measured on
      somebody else's distribution.

## Why this node exists

Retrieval is the part of a RAG system that determines its ceiling. If the right
document is not retrieved, nothing downstream can recover — and node 5.3 asks you
to attribute failures to retrieval or generation, which is impossible without a
retrieval number you trust.

It is also where "it looks fine" does the most damage, because retrieval results
usually do look fine. They are on topic. Being on topic is not the same as
containing the answer, and node 4.6 already showed you a system that scores 0.8
similarity on a contradiction.

## The session

Pick a corpus you can judge relevance in: your own notes, a documentation site,
a set of papers, this roadmap's own content. At least 500 chunks. You must be
able to tell whether a result is relevant, which rules out a domain you do not
know.

### 1. Chunk, embed, index

```python
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
model = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")     # 384-d, from node 4.6
```

Record your chunk size and overlap. They are parameters, not details.

Start with brute force — it is a matrix multiply, it is obviously correct, and at
500 chunks it is instant:

```python
scores = embeddings @ query_vector          # normalized, so this is cosine
top_k = np.argsort(-scores)[:k]
```

### 2. Build an evaluation set by hand

**30 query-document pairs, judged by you.**

Write realistic queries, run them, and mark which returned chunks are actually
relevant. Where nothing relevant came back, find the right chunk yourself and
record it.

This is tedious and it is the node. A retrieval system evaluated on its own
output is not evaluated.

### 3. Recall at three values of k

| | Dense | BM25 | Hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|
| recall@1 | | | |
| recall@5 | | | |
| recall@10 | | | |

`rank_bm25` gives you keyword search in three lines. Run it on the same 30
queries.

### 4. Find where each one wins

Three queries where BM25 beats dense. Three where dense beats BM25.

They will have obvious characters once you look: exact rare tokens against
paraphrase. Question 1 asks you to name them.

### 5. Hybrid

Combine the two rankings — reciprocal rank fusion is the standard and is about
five lines:

```python
score(doc) = sum over rankers of 1 / (60 + rank_in_that_ranker)
```

It should match or beat the better individual method. This is why production
retrieval is nearly always hybrid.

### 6. Chunk size, again

Re-chunk at half and double your original size, re-index, re-measure recall@5.

It will move. Question 3 asks what the trade-off is.

### 7. Approximate versus exact

Build a FAISS or hnswlib index. Measure recall against your brute-force results
and the speed difference.

At 500 chunks the speed difference is meaningless and the recall difference is
the interesting part. Question 4 asks when the trade flips.

### 8. Find a confident wrong answer

At least one query whose top result is on-topic and contradicts the query. Node
4.6 told you where to look: negations and antonyms.

That result is what node 5.3's generator will be handed, and it is the reason RAG
systems produce confidently wrong answers.

## Common walls

**Recall@1 equals recall@10.** Your queries are too easy, or too close to the
document text. Write queries the way a user would, not by copying phrases out of
the corpus.

**BM25 wins everywhere.** Your queries are all keyword-shaped. Write some
question-shaped ones.

**Your embeddings do not fit in memory.** 500 chunks at 384 dimensions is 750 KB.
If you are struggling, you are storing something else — probably the text — in
the same array.

**Everything scores above 0.7 cosine.** Normal, per node 4.6. Rankings matter,
absolute values do not.

## Before you move on

Node 5.3 assembles retrieval and generation into a system, and the first thing it
asks is which component a given failure belongs to. The recall numbers you just
measured are what make that question answerable.
