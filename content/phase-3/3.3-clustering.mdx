questions:
  - prompt: "Your inertia curve had no clear elbow and your silhouette peaked at k=15 when there are exactly 10 digits. Explain both, and say what you would actually do to choose k."
    answer_key: >-
      Inertia is the total squared distance from each point to its cluster centre,
      and adding a centre can only reduce it — at k equal to the number of points
      it reaches zero. So the curve always decreases and the elbow is a
      subjective bend rather than a computed answer; on high-dimensional data
      where distances concentrate, the bend often does not exist at all.
      Silhouette peaking at 15 rather than 10 is not a mistake: silhouette
      measures geometric separation, and several digits genuinely occupy multiple
      distinct clusters in pixel space — fours and nines written in different
      styles, ones with and without serifs — so splitting them produces tighter,
      better-separated groups. The number of clusters that best describes the
      geometry is simply not the number of semantic classes, and there is no
      reason it should be. What I would actually do: choose k from what the
      clusters are for. If they feed a marketing campaign with capacity for four
      segments, k is four. If they feed a downstream model, choose the k that
      maximises the downstream metric. Internal indices like silhouette are for
      sanity-checking a choice, not for making one.
  - prompt: "K-means produced five clusters from pure Gaussian noise with a positive silhouette score. Explain why, and describe how you would tell a real cluster structure from this."
    answer_key: >-
      K-means is not a test for whether clusters exist; it is a procedure that
      partitions data into k groups minimising within-group variance, and that
      procedure always succeeds. A cloud of Gaussian noise partitions perfectly
      well into five wedges, each of which is genuinely more compact than the
      whole, so silhouette is positive. Nothing in the algorithm or in the score
      can express "there is no structure here". To tell the difference you need a
      comparison against the null. The gap statistic does this directly, comparing
      within-cluster dispersion against what you would get on uniform data over
      the same range. More practically: run the clustering on shuffled or
      simulated null data and compare the scores — if your real data's silhouette
      is close to noise's, you have found nothing. Also check stability, since
      real structure is recovered consistently across seeds and subsamples while
      noise partitions differently each time, and check whether the clusters
      differ on variables you did not cluster on, which is the strongest evidence
      that they correspond to something outside the geometry.
  - prompt: "An adjusted Rand index of 0.4679 against the true digits — is that good? Justify your answer, and explain what the 'adjusted' is correcting for."
    answer_key: >-
      It depends entirely on what you expected. As a recovery of the ten digit
      classes it is poor — you would not use these clusters as digit predictions
      when a supervised model reaches well over 0.95 accuracy on the same data. As
      evidence that unsupervised structure in raw pixels corresponds substantially
      to semantic identity, having never been shown a single label, it is
      remarkable: a random assignment scores about 0.0. So the honest reading is
      that the clusters capture real structure and are not a substitute for
      labels. The "adjusted" corrects for chance agreement. The plain Rand index
      counts pairs of points that two labellings agree about, and with many
      clusters a large fraction of pairs are trivially in different groups under
      both, so the raw index is high even for random assignments and rises with
      the number of clusters. The adjusted version subtracts the expected value
      under a random model and rescales, so 0 means chance and 1 means identical,
      which makes it comparable across different numbers of clusters.
  - prompt: "You now have ten clusters of customers or digits. Describe how you would decide whether they are worth anything to somebody, without using any internal clustering metric."
    answer_key: >-
      Externally, in three ways. First, describe them: for each cluster, what is
      distinctive about it relative to the population — which features are far
      from the overall mean, and can you name the cluster in a sentence a
      non-technical colleague would recognise? A cluster you cannot describe is a
      cluster nobody can act on. Second, check they differ on something you did
      not cluster on. If you clustered on purchase behaviour and the clusters also
      differ sharply in support-ticket rate or retention, the structure is real
      and generalises beyond the variables you happened to choose. Third, and
      decisively, check that they change a decision: would you actually send these
      groups different messages, allocate different budgets, or route them
      differently? If the honest answer is that everyone gets the same treatment
      anyway, the clustering is an artefact of a project rather than an input to
      one. Stability across seeds and across time periods is the fourth thing
      worth testing, because a segmentation that reshuffles every quarter cannot
      be operationalised.

## Why this node exists

Clustering always works. That is the problem.

Every clustering algorithm will return clusters from any input, including data
with no structure whatsoever, and every internal metric will report a number.
Nothing in the standard workflow says "there is nothing here", so the
responsibility for noticing falls entirely on you.

This node makes you look at that directly, by clustering something with known
structure and then clustering something with none.

## The session

### 1. Digits, without their labels

```python
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
X = StandardScaler().fit_transform(load_digits().data)   # (1797, 64)
KMeans(n_clusters=10, random_state=0, n_init=10).fit(X)
```

| | |
|---|---|
| Inertia | 69405.5 |
| Silhouette | 0.1471 |
| Adjusted Rand index vs true digits | **0.4679** |

Never having seen a label, k-means recovers a substantial amount of the digit
structure. Also, 0.4679 is nowhere near enough to use as a classifier. Both
readings are correct and question 3 asks you to hold them together.

### 2. Choosing k, and failing to

Sweep k and plot both:

| k | Inertia | Silhouette |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 100427.3 | 0.108 |
| 3 | 93839.4 | 0.105 |
| 5 | 85099.6 | 0.103 |
| 8 | 75181.3 | 0.125 |
| 10 | 69405.5 | 0.147 |
| 15 | 60273.6 | **0.155** |
| 20 | 54101.7 | 0.154 |

Inertia falls smoothly with no elbow to find. Silhouette peaks at **15**, not at
10, even though the data contains exactly ten digits.

That is not a bug. Question 1 asks you to explain it, and the explanation is more
useful than any rule for picking k.

### 3. Cluster nothing

```python
noise = rng.normal(size=(1797, 64))
km = KMeans(n_clusters=5, random_state=0, n_init=10).fit(noise)
silhouette_score(noise, km.labels_)      # positive. every time.
```

There is no structure. K-means finds five clusters. The silhouette is positive.

Record the number and put it next to your real one. If they are close, your real
clustering has found nothing either — and that comparison is the single most
useful habit in this node.

### 4. Stability

Run k-means with five different `random_state` values and compute the adjusted
Rand index between each pair of runs. Real structure is recovered consistently.
Noise is not.

### 5. A different algorithm, with a different assumption

```python
DBSCAN(eps=..., min_samples=...)
```

DBSCAN finds dense regions of arbitrary shape, decides the number of clusters
itself, and — crucially — labels some points `-1`, meaning *not in any cluster*.

K-means structurally cannot do that. Every point gets assigned, including the
ones that belong to nothing. That difference matters far more than the accuracy
difference between the two.

## What each algorithm assumes

| Algorithm | Assumes | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| K-means | round, similar-sized, similar-density clusters | elongated or nested shapes, very different sizes |
| DBSCAN | clusters are dense regions separated by sparse ones | densities vary between clusters |
| Hierarchical | a tree of nested groupings is meaningful | you need flat clusters and must cut arbitrarily |
| Gaussian mixture | each cluster is a Gaussian blob | heavy tails, non-elliptical shapes |

Choosing an algorithm is choosing an assumption about shape. K-means being the
default is a historical accident, not a recommendation.

## Common walls

**One feature dominates every cluster.** You did not scale. K-means minimises
Euclidean distance, so a column measured in thousands drowns out one measured in
units — node 1.5's conditioning lesson in a new place.

**Your inertia curve has no elbow.** That is the normal case in high dimensions,
not a failure of your plotting. Distances concentrate as dimension rises, which
also depresses the silhouette — 0.147 is a low number in absolute terms and is
not the disaster it looks like for 64-dimensional data.

**Your clusters change completely between runs.** With `n_init=10` k-means takes
the best of ten initialisations, so genuine instability across seeds means the
structure is weak. Believe it.

**DBSCAN labels everything as noise, or everything as one cluster.** `eps` is
extremely sensitive and has no good default. Plot the distance to each point's
k-th nearest neighbour, sort it, and look for the knee.
